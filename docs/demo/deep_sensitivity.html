Deep Dive: Sensitivity Analysis — How Wrong Could We Be?

From Cornfield (1959) to the E-value to partial R² frontiers — the evolving toolkit for quantifying the fragility of causal claims to unmeasured confounding.

What Sensitivity Analysis Is — And Why Every Causal Claim Needs One

What. Sensitivity analysis answers: "How strong would an unmeasured confounder need to be to change my conclusion?" It quantifies the fragility of a causal estimate — not whether unmeasured confounding exists (that's untestable), but how much confounding would be required to nullify, reverse, or render statistically insignificant the observed effect.

Why. The assumption of "no unmeasured confounding" (ignorability) is fundamentally untestable from observational data. You can never prove you measured everything. Without sensitivity analysis, every observational causal claim is an open-loop assertion — precise but potentially precisely wrong. Sensitivity analysis closes the loop: it puts error bars on your assumptions, not just your sampling uncertainty.

Three reasons this matters:

  1. Untestable doesn't mean unquantifiable. You can't test whether unmeasured confounding is zero, but you CAN quantify how strong it would need to be. This transforms an untestable binary assumption into a calibratable continuous quantity.
  2. Not all "significant" results are robust. An estimate with p < 0.001 may be overturned by a confounder with RR = 1.5 with both treatment and outcome. An estimate with p = 0.04 may survive RR = 5.0 confounding. The p-value measures sampling uncertainty; sensitivity analysis measures structural uncertainty.
  3. Sensitivity analysis guides resource allocation. If your result is fragile (low E-value, low robustness value), you need to collect more covariates or design an experiment. If it's robust (high E-value), further sensitivity analysis may be unnecessary — the conclusion is structurally sound.
Everyday analogy — the suspicious lottery win: Your neighbor claims they won through skill. You suspect they simply bought more tickets than everyone else — but you can't observe ticket purchases (confounding by "tickets purchased"). Sensitivity asks: "How many MORE tickets would they need?" If they'd need 10,000× the legal limit — impossible — the skill claim survives. If only 2×, it crumbles. Limit: This analogy captures calibration against a known maximum (ticket limit) but treats the confounder as one-dimensional (number of tickets). Real confounders are multidimensional — they must correlate with BOTH treatment and outcome. A better version: the neighbor would need both extraordinary luck AND extraordinary ticket access — two conditions that must hold simultaneously.

1. The Untestable Assumption — Why Sensitivity Analysis Exists

Every observational causal estimate rests on ignorability (no unmeasured confounding):

This assumption cannot be verified from the data. You can check balance on observed covariates (SMD < 0.1), you can run placebo tests and negative controls — but you can never prove that no unmeasured confounder exists. The most you can do is quantify: if one does exist, how strong would it need to be to matter?

Key insight: Sensitivity analysis transforms the binary "identified or not?" question into a continuous "how fragile?" question. This is epistemologically more honest — it acknowledges that identification is always a matter of degree, not a binary property.

Precision in this distinction is precision in your uncertainty.

4. The Quantities Themselves — What RR, R², and Γ Actually Measure

Before deriving each method, we need to understand what the underlying quantities are — not just their formulas, but what kind of "thing" each one measures.

4.1 Risk Ratio (RR) — "How many times more likely?"

What it is. RR = P(Y=1 | T=1) / P(Y=1 | T=0). It's a multiplicative comparison of two probabilities. RR=2 means "treated units are twice as likely to experience the outcome." RR=1 means "no difference." RR=0.5 means "treated units are half as likely."

What kind of measure. RR is a ratio scale — it lives on (0, ∞), with 1 as the natural "no effect" reference point. It is NOT a distance (RR=4 is not "twice as far from 1" as RR=2 — the scale is multiplicative, not additive). Moving from RR=2 to RR=3 is a 50% increase in risk; moving from RR=10 to RR=11 is a 10% increase. Equal differences in RR do NOT represent equal differences in confounding strength.

What it captures. RR measures the strength of association between a binary variable and a binary outcome. When used in the E-value, RR_TU means "how much does U shift the probability of T?" and RR_UY means "how much does U shift the probability of Y?" Both are conditional on measured covariates — they are the residual association after accounting for X.

What RR=2.73 actually means in the world. If U has RR=2.73 with treatment T, then among people with the same measured covariates, those with U=1 are 2.73× as likely to receive treatment as those with U=0. If the baseline probability of treatment is 30%, the confounder shifts it to 30% × 2.73 ≈ 82% — a massive shift. This is why E-values above 2 are considered "robust": they require confounders that would dominate the treatment assignment process.

4.2 Partial R² — "What fraction of unexplained variance does it explain?"

What it is. R²_{T∼U|X} is the fraction of variance in T that U explains, AFTER X has already explained everything it can. If R² = 0.05, U explains 5% of what X left unexplained. This is fundamentally different from RR: it's on a [0,1] scale (a proportion), and it measures variance explained rather than probability ratio.

What kind of measure. Partial R² is a bounded proportion — it lives on [0,1], with 0 meaning "U explains nothing beyond X" and 1 meaning "U explains everything X didn't." It is additive across uncorrelated variables: if U₁ explains 3% and U₂ explains 4% and they're uncorrelated, together they explain ~7%. This makes it natural for thinking about multiple confounders.

What it captures. Partial R² measures the incremental predictive power of U — how much U improves our ability to predict T (or Y) beyond what X already achieves. It's a measure of residual dependence: after conditioning on X, how much "pattern" remains that U can capture?

What R²=0.05 actually means in the world. If X (income, age, education) already explains 40% of variance in treatment, and U explains 5% of the remaining 60%, then U captures 5% × 60% = 3% of the total variance. This is a small but real effect — comparable to adding one moderately predictive covariate to a regression. In social science contexts, R²=0.05 for a single variable is fairly typical; R²=0.20 would be considered very strong.

4.3 Rosenbaum's Γ — "How much can hidden bias tilt the playing field?"

What it is. Γ ≥ 1 bounds the odds ratio of treatment assignment between two matched units with identical observed covariates: 1/Γ ≤ (π_i/(1−π_i)) / (π_j/(1−π_j)) ≤ Γ. Γ=1 means perfectly random assignment within matched pairs. Γ=2 means one unit can be up to twice as likely (in odds) to receive treatment as its matched twin.

What kind of measure. Γ is a multiplicative bound — like RR, it lives on [1,∞) with 1 as the "no bias" reference. But unlike RR, Γ does NOT measure association with an outcome — it measures the maximum allowable distortion in the treatment assignment process. It's a measure of departure from random assignment, not a measure of confounding strength per se.

What it captures. Γ captures the design quality — how close your matched study is to a randomized experiment. A study that survives Γ=5 has a much stronger design than one that fails at Γ=1.2. In this sense, Γ is a design robustness metric: it tells you how much hidden bias your design can tolerate before the conclusion changes.

What Γ=2 actually means in the world. In a matched study of notification→order, two users with identical W, rain, weekend, payday should have the same notification probability. Γ=2 means: an unmeasured confounder could make one user up to 2× more likely (in odds) to receive a notification than the other — doubling the odds. If the baseline notification probability is 50% (odds=1), doubling the odds to 2 means probability rises to 67%. A confounder that can shift probability by 17 percentage points within matched pairs is substantial but not implausible — which is why Γ=2 is often considered a moderate sensitivity threshold.

Choosing the right sensitivity method means choosing the right scale for your problem.

4.5 Why These Formulas — First-Principles Justification

It's not enough to know what these quantities are. We need to understand why these specific mathematical structures — ratios, squares, square roots, products — are the right ones for the meanings we assign them.

Why a RATIO for RR? Probability is a measure on [0,1]. The difference P(Y|T=1) − P(Y|T=0) is bounded by the baseline: if P(Y|T=0)=0.8, the maximum possible difference is 0.2. But the RATIO P(Y|T=1)/P(Y|T=0) has no such bound — it can be arbitrarily large. This matters for confounding: a confounder that shifts probability from 0.01 to 0.05 (difference=0.04, ratio=5) may be more important than one that shifts from 0.50 to 0.60 (difference=0.10, ratio=1.2). The ratio captures multiplicative effect size, which is more relevant when baseline probabilities vary across strata. The ratio is also the natural scale for the Ding-VanderWeele bound because the bound's derivation uses the factorization P(Y|T) = Σ_u P(Y|T,u)P(u|T) — a weighted average whose multiplicative structure emerges from the law of total probability applied to conditional probabilities. The ratio preserves this multiplicative structure; the difference does not.

Why a SQUARE for R²? Variance is defined as E[(X−μ)²] — the expected squared deviation from the mean. The SQUARE is not an arbitrary choice: it comes from the geometry of projection. In linear algebra, the residual vector ê = Y − Xβ is orthogonal to the column space of X (the normal equations: Xᵀê = 0). The squared length ‖ê‖² is the residual sum of squares. The proportion ‖Xβ‖²/‖Y‖² (after centering) is R² — the fraction of total squared length explained by the model. The SQUARE is essential here because: (i) orthogonality (uncorrelatedness) is defined through inner products, which involve squares, (ii) the Pythagorean theorem ‖Y‖² = ‖Xβ‖² + ‖ê‖² holds only with squared lengths — this is what makes R² a proper fraction (the parts sum to the whole), (iii) the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem, which underpins the OVB formula, relies on projection geometry where squared norms are the natural measure. Without squares, the decomposition doesn't close.

Why the PRODUCT RR_TU × RR_UY in the E-value? Confounding requires U to be associated with BOTH T and Y. If either association is zero, there is no confounding — regardless of how strong the other is. The PRODUCT captures this logical AND: both must be non-zero for confounding to exist. Moreover, the Ding-VanderWeele bound shows that the maximum bias is proportional to the product divided by the sum — the product appears in the numerator because confounding strength multiplies (U→T association routes through T; U→Y association routes through Y; their joint effect on the T-Y association is multiplicative), while the sum appears in the denominator because the total association is constrained by the marginal probabilities (you cannot have both RRs arbitrarily large simultaneously — they compete for the same probability mass). This product-sum structure is not a modeling choice; it's forced by the laws of probability.

Why the SQUARE ROOT in the E-value formula? The E-value formula E = RR_obs + √(RR_obs(RR_obs−1)) contains a square root. Where does it come from? The Ding-VanderWeele bound gives RR_obs ≤ e²/(2e−1) under the worst-case assumption RR_TU = RR_UY = e. Rearranging: RR_obs(2e−1) ≤ e² → e² − 2·RR_obs·e + RR_obs ≤ 0. This is a quadratic inequality in e. Solving the quadratic equation e² − 2·RR_obs·e + RR_obs = 0 gives e = RR_obs ± √(RR_obs² − RR_obs) = RR_obs ± √(RR_obs(RR_obs−1)). The larger root (with the + sign) is the E-value — it's the minimum e that satisfies the inequality. The square root emerges from the quadratic formula, which itself emerges from the algebraic structure of the Ding-VanderWeele bound. The square root is not inserted for convenience — it is the mathematical consequence of asking "what is the smallest e such that e²/(2e−1) ≥ RR_obs?" The quadratic structure, in turn, comes from the product-sum form of the bound: e² in the numerator (product of two e's) and 2e−1 in the denominator (sum of two e's, minus overlap).

Why an ODDS RATIO for Γ? The odds π/(1−π) transforms probability from [0,1] to [0,∞) — an unbounded scale. This is essential because Γ must be able to represent arbitrarily large distortions (a confounder could, in principle, make treatment practically certain for some units and practically impossible for others). The odds ratio is also the natural measure for matched designs because: (i) in a matched pair, the difference in log-odds is the sufficient statistic for the treatment effect under a logistic model, (ii) the odds ratio is invariant to the marginal probability of treatment — it measures association within the pair, not across the population, (iii) the permutation distribution of the test statistic under the null depends on the odds ratio through the propensity score, and bounding the odds ratio directly bounds the possible permutation distributions. The RATIO of odds (not the difference) is used because multiplicative distortions in assignment probability correspond to additive shifts on the log-odds scale — the natural scale for binary regression.

5. The E-value — Minimum Confounding Strength (VanderWeele & Ding 2017)

5.1 Semantic Interpretation

The E-value answers: "What is the minimum risk ratio an unmeasured confounder must have with BOTH treatment and outcome, conditional on measured covariates, to fully explain away the observed association?"

Think of it as a "worst-case" benchmark. An E-value of 2.73 means: even in the most pessimistic scenario, a confounder would need RR ≥ 2.73 with both T and Y to reduce the true effect to zero. If domain experts believe no unmeasured confounder could be that strong, the result is robust.

Everyday analogy — the two-skill safe-cracker: A confounder must be strong in two dimensions simultaneously — associated with BOTH treatment AND outcome. Think of a safe that requires two hands turning two dials at once. The E-value says: "To crack this safe, a thief would need to apply at least 273 lbs of pressure with EACH hand simultaneously." Your strongest friend can do 180 lbs with one hand — and almost no one can sustain that with both. The safe isn't provably uncrackable, but the thief would need to be bilaterally implausible. This captures the E-value's key structure: RR with T AND RR with Y, both ≥ E-value.
Where this analogy breaks — and what the E-value CANNOT do: (1) Multiple thieves. The E-value assumes a SINGLE confounder. Two weak confounders acting together could theoretically crack the safe even when neither alone meets the threshold — the safe's two-hand test doesn't rule out a team. (2) Different methods of entry. The E-value measures force (RR scale). If the confounder operates through a different mechanism — e.g., an instrument-like variable that affects T through a channel the E-value's derivation doesn't cover — the analogy fails. (3) You might not know the strongest person alive. "Benchmarks" (strongest known confounder, strongest person you know) calibrate against your current knowledge, not against reality. Before 1950, "smoking→lung cancer: RR≈10-20" would have sounded absurd — but it was real. The E-value tells you a confounder would need to be smoking-strength; it does NOT tell you smoking-strength confounders don't exist. That judgment requires domain expertise, not math.

Step 4 — Why the math means what we claim: the logical chain. The E-value is not just a formula — it's the endpoint of a logical argument. Here is why each step is valid and what it guarantees:

4a. Why the Ding-VanderWeele bound holds. The inequality RR_obs ≤ RR_true × (RR_TU × RR_UY)/(RR_TU + RR_UY − 1) is not an approximation — it's a theorem. Ding & VanderWeele (2016, Epidemiology) proved it from the definitions of risk ratios and the law of total probability, assuming only that U is binary or polytomous and that the measured covariates X are sufficient to block confounding from U. The proof works by: (i) expressing the observed RR as a weighted average of stratum-specific true RRs, (ii) showing that the weights are constrained by RR_TU and RR_UY, (iii) maximizing the possible bias over all distributions of U consistent with those constraints. The result is a sharp bound — there exists some distribution of U that achieves it, so it cannot be tightened without additional assumptions.

4b. Why RR_true = 1 is the right "worst case." We're asking: "Could confounding explain away the ENTIRE effect?" Setting RR_true = 1 means "there is zero true causal effect — everything we observe is confounding." If even THIS extreme scenario requires an implausibly strong confounder, then any PARTIAL confounding (RR_true > 1 but smaller than observed) requires an even stronger confounder to close the remaining gap. Setting RR_true = 1 gives the minimum confounder strength needed — it's the easiest case for the confounder to win. If the confounder can't win even in this easiest case, it certainly can't win in harder cases.

4c. Why RR_TU = RR_UY = e is conservative. The Ding-VanderWeele bound involves the product RR_TU × RR_UY. For a fixed product, the ratio (RR_TU × RR_UY)/(RR_TU + RR_UY − 1) is MAXIMIZED when RR_TU = RR_UY. So assuming equality gives the largest possible bias for a given product — the worst case. Any real confounder with unequal associations would produce LESS bias for the same product, or equivalently, would need a LARGER product to achieve the same bias. The E-value uses this worst case, so it's conservative: a real confounder would need to be even stronger than the E-value suggests.

4d. What the math guarantees — and what it doesn't. The E-value guarantees: IF an unmeasured confounder U exists, AND U is sufficient to explain away the entire observed association (RR_true = 1), THEN U must have RR ≥ E-value with both T and Y. The E-value does NOT guarantee: that such a U actually exists, that no U exists (even if E-value is large), or that the observed association is causal (even if E-value is enormous). The math is a conditional statement: it tells you what must be true IF confounding explains everything. Whether that conditional's antecedent is plausible is a domain judgment, not a mathematical one.

4e. Boundary conditions — when the math stops working. The E-value derivation assumes: (i) U is a single binary/polytomous variable, (ii) measured covariates X are sufficient to block all other confounding paths (only U is unmeasured), (iii) the risk ratio is the correct scale, (iv) positivity holds. It does NOT apply when: multiple unmeasured confounders act jointly (their combined effect could exceed the E-value even if each individually doesn't), the outcome is continuous (use partial R² instead), or the treatment effect is heterogeneous in ways correlated with U.

6. The Partial R² Framework — How Much Variance Does the Confounder Explain? (Cinelli & Hazlett 2020)

4.1 Semantic Interpretation

Instead of risk ratios, the partial R² framework asks: "What fraction of the residual variance in treatment and outcome must a confounder explain to change the conclusion?" This has two key advantages over the E-value: (1) it naturally handles continuous treatments and outcomes, and (2) it allows benchmarking against observed covariates ("a confounder 3× as strong as income would be needed").

Everyday analogy — the "how much stronger than income?" test: Imagine you're debating whether a raise actually improves productivity, or whether "motivation" (which you didn't measure) confounds both. A colleague says: "Motivation is probably a confounder." You reply: "OK — how much stronger than income would motivation need to be to overturn my result?" Income explains 8% of the variance in getting a raise. The partial R² framework tells you: motivation would need to explain 24% — three times as much as income. Now your colleague has to defend whether an unmeasured variable could really be 3× as predictive as income. The burden of proof shifts — from you having to prove there's NO confounder, to your critic having to argue that a confounder that strong could exist. This is the power of benchmarking: it makes the debate concrete and calibratable.

Why the math means what we claim — the logical chain for Partial R²:

Why the OVB formula captures confounding. The formula Bias(τ̂) = δ × γ / (1 − R²_{T∼X}) is derived from the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem: in a linear model, the coefficient on T equals the coefficient from regressing Y (residualized on X) on T (residualized on X). When U is omitted, the residualized T still contains the part of U that predicts T (δ), and the residualized Y contains the part of U that predicts Y (γ). Their product δ×γ is exactly the confounding bias — it's the spurious correlation between T and Y induced by their shared dependence on U. The denominator 1/(1 − R²_{T∼X}) accounts for the fact that including X reduces the residual variance in T, which amplifies the relative importance of the omitted U.

Why benchmarking against observed covariates is valid. The partial R² of an observed covariate (say, income) is computed from the SAME data and the SAME model as the hypothetical unmeasured confounder would use. If income explains R² = 0.03 of residual treatment variance, and the RV says you need R² = 0.09, then you're saying: "the confounder must explain 3× as much variance as income." This is valid because: (i) both are computed on the same scale (partial R²), (ii) both are conditional on the same set of already-included covariates X, (iii) the comparison is transitive — if income is the strongest thing you CAN measure, and the confounder must be 3× stronger than that, then either the confounder is extraordinarily powerful or income is a weak benchmark. Either way, the comparison is honest.

What the math guarantees — and what it doesn't. The partial R² framework guarantees: IF a confounder U exists with partial R² values (R²_T, R²_Y), THEN the bias is exactly δ×γ/(1−R²_{T∼X}), which can be expressed in terms of (R²_T, R²_Y). It does NOT guarantee: that the linear model is correctly specified (if the true relationships are nonlinear, the partial R² may misstate the confounder's importance), that the confounder's effect is additive (interactions with T would change the bias formula), or that the observed covariates are sufficient to identify the other coefficients (they may be colliders). Extent: The framework holds exactly for linear models with additive confounding. For nonlinear models, it provides a useful approximation whose accuracy depends on how close to linear the true relationships are. For binary outcomes, the E-value is generally preferred; for continuous outcomes, partial R² is the natural choice.

7. Rosenbaum Bounds — Sensitivity for Matched Designs (Rosenbaum 2002)

5.1 Semantic Interpretation

Rosenbaum bounds ask: "How large would the departure from random assignment need to be (in terms of odds ratios) to render my significant result non-significant?" This is the classic sensitivity analysis for matched observational studies.

Everyday analogy — the twins study: You compare identical twins where one smoked and the other didn't. You match on genetics, upbringing, diet — everything you can measure. But what if the smoking twin was also more stressed (unmeasured)? Rosenbaum's Γ quantifies: "Even if the stressed twin was up to 2× more likely to smoke, my result still holds." Γ = 2 means you can tolerate a confounder that doubles the odds of treatment; Γ = 5 means your result survives even a 5× odds multiplier. The sensitivity limit Γ* is the breaking point: the largest Γ for which you can still reject the null. If Γ* = 3.5, your result survives anything up to a 3.5× odds departure from random assignment — a confounder stronger than that would be needed to overturn your finding. This is the matched-design analog of the E-value, and it works without assuming anything about the unmeasured confounder's distribution.

5.2 Mathematical Derivation

In a matched pair design, two units i and j with the same observed covariates X have treatment assignment probabilities π_i and π_j. If assignment were random (no unmeasured confounding), π_i = π_j. Rosenbaum introduces Γ ≥ 1 bounding the odds ratio:

Γ = 1 ⇒ random assignment (no confounding). Γ = 2 ⇒ matched individuals could differ in treatment odds by up to 2× due to an unmeasured U. For each Γ, compute the range of possible p-values. The sensitivity limit Γ* is the largest Γ for which the result remains significant at α = 0.05.

Why the math means what we claim — the logical chain for Rosenbaum bounds:

Why Γ bounds the odds ratio of assignment. In a matched pair (i, j) with identical observed covariates X_i = X_j, if assignment were random, the odds of treatment would be equal: π_i/(1−π_i) = π_j/(1−π_j). The parameter Γ ≥ 1 is the maximum factor by which an unmeasured confounder U can distort this equality. If Γ = 2, then even if U makes unit i up to 2× more likely to be treated than unit j (or vice versa), we can still bound the distribution of the test statistic. The key insight: Γ does not assume anything about U's distribution, strength, or even existence — it simply says "whatever U is, its effect on the odds ratio is at most Γ."

Why the p-value bound is valid. For a given Γ, Rosenbaum computes the range of possible p-values over ALL distributions of U consistent with the Γ-bound on the odds ratio. This is a worst-case analysis: if the maximum possible p-value (under the most adverse U allowed by Γ) is still below α = 0.05, then NO confounder bounded by Γ can overturn the result. The computation uses the fact that under the null, the test statistic's distribution is bounded by two known distributions (the permutation distributions under the extreme scenarios where U maximally favors treatment in one group or the other).

What the math guarantees — and what it doesn't. Rosenbaum bounds guarantee: IF the odds ratio distortion from U is at most Γ, THEN the p-value is in the computed range. They do NOT guarantee: that Γ = 1 (random assignment) is true, that the matched design successfully balanced unobserved covariates (only observed ones), or that the test statistic is the most powerful one available. Extent: The bounds are sharp for the given Γ — there exists a distribution of U that achieves the bound. But they are conservative in the sense that the actual U may be far less adversarial than the worst case. If your result survives Γ = 3, it would also survive any real confounder weaker than that.

8. The Breakdown Frontier — The Full Sensitivity Surface (Masten & Poirier 2021)

6.1 Semantic Interpretation

Rather than a single number (E-value, RV), the breakdown frontier maps the entire boundary of (R²_{T∼U}, R²_{Y∼U}) combinations that would nullify the result. It answers: "What are ALL the confounder profiles that could explain away my effect?"

Everyday analogy — the "shape of danger": The E-value tells you the confounder needs RR ≥ 2.73 with BOTH T and Y. But what if the confounder is extremely predictive of the outcome (R² = 0.50) but only weakly predictive of treatment (R² = 0.01)? The E-value treats these symmetrically and might say "safe." But the breakdown frontier shows that this asymmetric confounder ALSO overturns your result — it just does so from a different direction. Think of the frontier as the "danger curve" on a map: everything above the curve is dangerous (would overturn your result), everything below is safe. The E-value and RV are single points on this curve — useful summaries, but they don't show you the full shape of the risk. A result might survive a symmetric confounder (E-value says "safe") but be vulnerable to an asymmetric one (frontier says "danger above this curve"). The frontier gives you the complete picture.

6.2 Why a Single Number Is Not Enough

Consider two confounders: U₁ has R²_{T∼U}=0.01 but R²_{Y∼U}=0.50 (very weak with T, very strong with Y). U₂ has R²_{T∼U}=0.50 but R²_{Y∼U}=0.01 (strong with T, weak with Y). The E-value treats them symmetrically (worst case). The frontier shows that BOTH can be damaging — and which regions of the (R²_T, R²_Y) space are "dangerous" depends on the study design.

The frontier is a hyperbola-like curve in (R²_T, R²_Y) space. Points above the curve correspond to confounders that would overturn the result. The E-value is one point on this curve (where R²_T = R²_Y). The robustness value is a perpendicular projection. The frontier is the complete characterization.

9. How Each Method Handles Unmeasured vs. Unknown — Worked Examples

8.1 The E-value — Handles BOTH Without Distinction

How it deals with unmeasured: You suspect stress confounds your notification→order estimate but didn't measure it. The E-value says: "ANY confounder, whether it's stress or something else, needs RR≥2.73 with both T and Y." Since you know from the literature that stress→smoking RR≈1.5 (weaker than 2.73), even stress couldn't explain away your result. The E-value doesn't need you to name the confounder — it works exactly the same for unmeasured and unknown.

How it deals with unknown: A critic says "maybe there's a variable you've never thought of." The E-value responds: "That variable would need RR≥2.73 with both T and Y. The strongest risk factor in this entire domain (smoking→lung cancer) has RR≈10-20. Most behavioral variables have RR<2.0. So your unknown confounder would need to be unprecedentedly strong." The E-value is the only method that treats unmeasured and unknown identically — it needs no calibration in either case.

How it deals with multiple confounders: The E-value assumes a SINGLE unmeasured confounder. If multiple confounders exist, their joint effect could be stronger than any single one. VanderWeele & Ding note this limitation: the E-value is conservative for a single U, but multiple weak confounders acting together could theoretically explain an effect even when no single one exceeds the E-value. Rule of thumb: if the E-value is large (>3) and domain experts cannot name even ONE plausible confounder that strong, multiple confounders are unlikely to jointly explain the effect — but this is a judgment call, not a mathematical guarantee.

8.2 Partial R² / Robustness Value — Excels at Unmeasured, Struggles with Unknown

How it deals with unmeasured: This is where Partial R² shines. You didn't measure "motivation" but you DID measure "income." Income explains 8% of residual variance in treatment. The RV says you need a confounder explaining 24%. You can definitively state: "Motivation would need to be 3× as strong as income." This calibrates the debate — your critic must now argue that motivation is 3× more predictive than income, which is a concrete, disputable claim.

How it deals with unknown: Without a named variable, you can't calibrate against observed covariates — you don't know what to compare to. You can only benchmark against the maximum observed partial R² among ALL measured covariates: "The strongest measured variable explains 12%; an unknown confounder would need to explain 24% — twice the maximum." This is weaker than a named-variable calibration but still informative. Bottom line: Partial R² is strongest when you can name the confounder; for unknown confounders, prefer the E-value.

How it deals with multiple confounders: The framework naturally extends to multiple confounders via the partial R² of the set {U₁, U₂, ..., U_k} jointly. The RV asks about the minimum variance a confounder SET must explain. This is an advantage over the E-value: you can bound the joint explanatory power of multiple unmeasured variables, not just a single one.

8.3 Rosenbaum Γ — Design-Based, Handles Both Through Odds Ratios

How it deals with unmeasured: In a matched study of notification→order, two users with identical W, rain, weekend, payday should have equal notification probability. Γ = 2 means: even if an unmeasured confounder (stress) made one user up to 2× more likely to receive a notification, the result still holds. You're saying: "I can tolerate a confounder that doubles the odds of treatment, which is stronger than any measured covariate's effect on treatment."

How it deals with unknown: Γ doesn't require naming the confounder — it only bounds the odds ratio of assignment. An unknown confounder with effect up to Γ on the odds ratio cannot overturn your result. Like the E-value, Rosenbaum bounds are calibration-free for unknown confounders.

How it deals with multiple confounders: Γ bounds the COMBINED effect of all unmeasured confounders on treatment assignment odds. This is an advantage: it handles multiple confounders naturally — you don't need to assume a single U.

8.4 Breakdown Frontier — Maps the Full Risk Landscape for Unknown Confounders

How it deals with unknown: This is the frontier's strongest use case. You cannot name the confounder, so you cannot calibrate. The frontier shows you the COMPLETE set of (R²_T, R²_Y) pairs that would overturn your result. You can then check: does any KNOWN variable in any related field fall in the danger zone? If not, an unknown confounder would need to be stronger than anything ever documented. The frontier turns "unknown" into "unprecedentedly strong" — which is as close to a refutation as you can get for an unknown unknown.

How it deals with multiple confounders: Like Partial R², the frontier naturally handles confounder sets. The R² values are for the JOINT effect of all unmeasured variables. The frontier shows the minimum joint explanatory power needed.

11. The Cornfield Conditions — Where It All Began (1959)

Cornfield et al. (1959) addressed Fisher's claim that a genetic confounder could explain the smoking-lung cancer association. They showed: for a confounder to fully explain the observed RR, its association with the outcome must be at least as strong as the observed association itself. Specifically, if smoking has RR=9 for lung cancer, any confounder must have RR ≥ 9 with lung cancer — stronger than almost any known risk factor. This was the first sensitivity analysis in history, and it convinced the scientific community that smoking causes cancer.

Historical significance: Cornfield's inequality is the intellectual ancestor of the E-value. The E-value generalizes it to arbitrary RR values and proves it as a mathematical bound (the Ding-VanderWeele inequality), not just a heuristic.

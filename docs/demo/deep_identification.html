Deep Dive: Causal Identification — Σ (Summing Out) as the Universal Engine

Why every causal identification method — back-door, front-door, IV, do-calculus — is fundamentally the same operation: conditioning + marginalization over non-targeted variables.

What Identification Is — And Why It Matters

What. Identification is the problem of expressing a causal quantity (something involving do(T) or potential outcomes Y(t)) purely in terms of observable probabilities — things you can actually compute from data. Mathematically:

The Σ (sum / integral / marginalization) is the operator that makes this translation possible. Every identification method — back-door, front-door, IV, DiD, RDD, do-calculus — is a specific recipe for what to condition on and what to sum over.

Why. Without identification, you are doing statistics, not causal inference. You can have the best estimator in the world — if the quantity you're estimating is not the causal effect, you're precisely estimating the wrong thing. Identification is prior to and separate from estimation (Pearl 2009, §3; Hernán & Robins 2020, Part II). Three reasons this matters:

  1. No statistical sophistication rescues a non-identified estimand. You cannot "deep learn" your way out of confounding. If P(Y|do(T)) is not expressible in terms of P(Y|T,X), no estimator — no matter how flexible or well-regularized — will recover the truth.
  2. Identification determines the adjustment set — not the data. The DAG tells you which variables to condition on. The data cannot tell you this. "Just control for everything" guarantees mediator bias, collider bias, or both (Cinelli, Forney & Pearl 2022).
  3. Every identification formula is a Σ. Once you see this, the entire landscape of causal methods unifies: back-door adjustment = Σ over confounders; front-door = nested Σ over mediator + treatment; IV = implicit Σ over compliance types; DiD = Σ over time×group cells. The methods differ in what you sum over, never in whether you sum.

Key insight. Identification is the bridge between Rung 1 (association, P(Y|T)) and Rung 2 (intervention, P(Y|do(T))). The bridge is built from assumptions (the DAG) and crossed by Σ (marginalization). Without the assumptions, you cannot build the bridge. Without Σ, you cannot cross it. Both are necessary; neither is sufficient alone. This is why the project's Design Principle P1 insists that assumptions are first-class artifacts — they are the foundation the bridge stands on.

Every identification method is a specific recipe for which variables to condition on and which to marginalize over — but the operation itself is always a sum.

2. The Mathematical Structure — Why Σ?

2.1 The Law of Total Probability as the Foundation

Every identification formula traces back to the law of total probability:

Given a partition {Z} of the sample space, we can "integrate out" Z to recover the marginal distribution of Y. In causal inference, we use this law after conditioning on the right variables — the art is choosing Z such that the conditional probabilities become "do-free" (expressible in observational terms).

2.2 Truncated Factorization → Marginalization

Pearl's truncated factorization (Pearl 2009, Ch.3) is the general mechanism:

After deleting the equation for T and fixing T=t, we marginalize over all variables except Y. The back-door, front-door, and IV formulas are special cases of this general marginalization — they just take advantage of graph structure to simplify which variables actually need to be summed over.

2.3 Why Not Just "Control for Everything"?

The temptation is to write: P(Y|do(T)) = Σ_all P(Y|T, all_vars) P(all_vars). This fails because:

  1. Conditioning on a mediator (M) blocks the causal path T→M→Y — you measure the direct effect, not the total effect.
  2. Conditioning on a collider (S where T→S←Y) OPENS a non-causal path — Berkson's bias.
  3. Conditioning on an instrument (Z→T only) amplifies residual confounding bias (Pearl 2011).

The back-door criterion (and its generalizations) tells you exactly which Z to sum over — neither more nor less. The Σ is cheap; the hard part is choosing the right conditioning set.

4. The Back-Door Criterion — Detailed Derivation

4.1 The Criterion (Pearl 1995, 2009 §3.3.1)

A set of variables Z satisfies the back-door criterion relative to (T, Y) if:

  1. No descendant of T is in Z. This ensures we don't block causal paths or open collider paths via mediators.
  2. Z d-separates T from Y in the back-door graph. The back-door graph is the original DAG with all edges out of T deleted.

If Z satisfies the criterion, the causal effect is identified by the back-door adjustment formula:

4.2 Why the Sum Works — Step by Step

Step 1 — Markov factorization: P(V) = ∏_j P(V_j | pa(V_j)). The joint distribution factorizes over the DAG.

Step 2 — Apply do(T=t): Delete P(T|pa(T)), fix T=t. The truncated factorization is P(V \ {T} | do(T=t)) = ∏_{V_j≠T} P(V_j | pa(V_j)) |_{T=t}.

Step 3 — Marginalize over Z (the adjustment set): P(Y | do(T=t)) = Σ_z P(Y | T=t, Z=z) P(Z=z). This Σ is the key operation. It "integrates out" the confounders Z, weighted by their population prevalence P(Z=z). The result is a weighted average of stratum-specific associations — which, because Z blocks all back-door paths, equals the causal effect.

4.3 Graph Surgery Algorithm

Find-Adjustment-Set(G, T, Y):
1. Form the back-door graph GBD by deleting all edges out of T.
2. Let C = all observed non-descendants of T.
3. For each subset Z ⊆ C (smallest first): if Z d-separates T from Y in GBD, return Z.
4. If no subset works: not back-door identifiable — try front-door, IV, or general ID.

5.3 do-Calculus — The Complete Marginalization Calculus

Shpitser & Pearl (2008) proved that the ID algorithm is complete: any identifiable causal effect can be expressed as a sequence of Σ operations. The three rules of do-calculus (Pearl 1995, 2009 §3.4) are:

Rule 1 (Insertion/deletion of observations): If Y and Z are d-separated by X and W in GX̅ (the graph with arrows INTO X removed), then you can add/remove Z from the conditioning set.

Rule 2 (Action/observation exchange): If Y and Z are d-separated by X and W in GX̅Z̲ (the graph with arrows INTO X removed AND arrows OUT OF Z removed), then P(Y|do(X),do(Z),W) = P(Y|do(X),Z,W) — you can replace do(Z) with see(Z).

Rule 3 (Insertion/deletion of actions): If Y and Z are d-separated by X and W in GX̅,Z(W)̅, then you can add/remove do(Z).

Each rule licenses a Σ operation: Rule 1 allows introducing a variable to sum over; Rule 2 allows replacing a do() with a condition (which makes Σ possible); Rule 3 allows removing irrelevant interventions. Every step of the ID algorithm is an application of one of these three rules, and every application either introduces a Σ or eliminates a do().

6. Beyond the Canonical Trio — Other Identification Strategies

6.1 Difference-in-Differences (DiD)

DiD identifies the ATT under the parallel trends assumption: in the absence of treatment, the treated and control groups would have followed parallel trajectories. The Σ here is implicit — it operates over time periods and groups:

The identification is not through conditioning on confounders but through differencing out time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity. The Σ sums over units within each group×time cell.

6.2 Regression Discontinuity Design (RDD)

RDD identifies a local causal effect at a cutoff by exploiting a discontinuous jump in treatment probability:

The Σ here sums over units within a bandwidth of the cutoff. Identification relies on continuity of potential outcomes at the threshold — not on conditioning on confounders. This is a fundamentally different Σ: it marginalizes over a local region rather than over a global adjustment set.

6.3 Synthetic Control

Synthetic control identifies the ATT by constructing a weighted combination of untreated units (the "synthetic control") that matches the treated unit's pre-treatment trajectory. The Σ is a constrained weighted sum — the weights are chosen to minimize pre-treatment outcome differences, not to satisfy a back-door criterion. This is Σ as optimization rather than Σ as marginalization.

7. Critical Analysis — What Σ Can and Cannot Do

6.1 What Σ Achieves

The marginalization operation is necessary and sufficient for causal identification: every identified causal effect is a Σ over some set, and every Σ over a valid adjustment set identifies a causal effect. This is a deep theorem (Shpitser & Pearl 2006, Theorem 4). The Σ is the only bridge between the interventional and observational distributions.

6.2 What Σ Cannot Do

  • Σ cannot create information. If the causal effect is not identified (the adjustment set does not exist), no amount of summing can produce a valid estimate. The Σ is an unbiased transformer of existing information, not a generator of new information.
  • Σ cannot rescue a misspecified DAG. If the graph is wrong — if you condition on a collider thinking it's a confounder, or omit a true confounder — the Σ produces a precisely wrong answer. The weighted average it computes is causally meaningless.
  • Σ cannot handle unmeasured confounding in the back-door case. If a confounder U is unobserved AND no proxy/mediator/instrument is available, the Σ over the observed variables does NOT equal P(Y|do(T)). This is the fundamental limitation: the Σ can only integrate out variables you have.
  • Σ assumes positivity. The sum requires P(Z=z) > 0 for all z, and P(T=t | Z=z) strictly between 0 and 1. Violations make the Σ undefined — you cannot average over empty strata.

6.3 The Epistemological Status of the Σ

Cartwright (1999) argued that "no causes in, no causes out" — the DAG encodes causal assumptions that no amount of data or Σ can replace. The Σ is a truth-preserving transformation: if the DAG is correct, Σ gives the right answer; if the DAG is wrong, Σ gives a wrong answer with the appearance of mathematical rigor.

6.4 A Serious Counter-Position — "Probabilistic Modelling Is Sufficient"

Mlodozeniec, Krueger & Turner (2025, ICML Oral) advance a provocative thesis that directly challenges the Pearlian framework: probabilistic modelling alone is sufficient for causal inference — no specialized do-operator, SCMs, or do-calculus required. Their argument, grounded in MacKay's principle ("always write down the probability of everything"), proceeds as follows:

  1. Joint model of all worlds. Construct a single probabilistic model (Bayesian network) spanning both the factual world (what happened) and hypothetical intervened worlds (what would happen under do(T=t)). The "twin network" approach (Balke & Pearl 1994) embeds counterfactuals in a standard graphical model.
  2. Causal queries reduce to probabilistic inference. With the full joint specified, interventional questions P(Y|do(T=t)) and counterfactual questions P(Y(0)|T=1,Y=1) become standard conditional/marginal queries — just Σ over the latent variables connecting the factual and counterfactual worlds.
  3. The do-operator is syntactic sugar. They reinterpret Pearl's do-calculus and SCMs not as fundamentally distinct mathematical operations, but as convenient notation for particular patterns of conditioning + marginalization within a larger probabilistic model. SCMs are recast as a restricted class of Bayesian networks with explicitly shared noise variables.
What this means for our Σ thesis: Mlodozeniec et al. actually support the core mathematical claim of this deep dive — that Σ (marginalization) is the universal engine. But they go further: they argue that the entire causal inference apparatus (DAGs, do-operator, identification criteria, do-calculus) is a notational layer on top of what is, fundamentally, just probabilistic modeling with Σ. The disagreement is not about the math (Σ is Σ) but about whether the specialized causal notation adds essential structure (Pearl's position: the DAG encodes untestable assumptions that probabilistic models alone cannot derive) or is merely convenient (Mlodozeniec et al.'s position: a sufficiently expressive probabilistic model already contains all the necessary structure).

Our assessment: Both positions agree that Σ is the engine. The disagreement is epistemological: where do the assumptions that determine what to Σ over come from? Pearl says they come from the DAG — an external causal theory. Mlodozeniec et al. say they come from the joint probabilistic model itself — you specify P(everything) and Σ does the rest. In practice, specifying P(everything) for a real problem requires encoding exactly the same domain knowledge that a DAG encodes, just in a different formalism. The DAG makes assumptions visible and falsifiable (every absent edge is a testable claim); a monolithic joint model may bury the same assumptions in prior and structure choices that are harder to audit. The value of the causal framework, then, is not that it enables something probabilistically impossible — it's that it makes assumptions explicit, versioned, and testable — which is exactly Design Principle P1 of this project.

The critical difference: Bayesian Σ uses P(Z | T) if you condition on T, or P(Z) if you don't. The causal Σ deliberately uses P(Z) — the unconditional distribution — even though T is in the model. This is exactly the difference between "seeing" (Rung 1) and "doing" (Rung 2): the intervention do(T=t) cuts the arrow into T, so Z's distribution is its natural P(Z), not the treatment-conditional P(Z|T). The Σ is the same; what changes is the weight.

7.2 The Math Bridge Connection — Why Bayes Never Leaves Rung 1

The Math Bridge curriculum (Levels 1-3) demonstrates this numerically:

  • Level 1 (Bayesian Inference): Metropolis-Hastings from scratch correctly computes P(θ | data) — the posterior over parameters. But when used to estimate a treatment effect in the presence of confounding, the posterior concentrates on the wrong value. Bayes is doing the Σ correctly — it's just summing over the wrong weight.
  • Level 2 (Bayesian Networks): A BN is P(V) = ∏ P(V_j | pa(V_j)). The factorization is correct for the observational distribution. But P(Y | T) ≠ P(Y | do(T)) — the BN knows the joint, but doesn't know which arrows are causal.
  • Level 3 (The Causal Step): The truncated factorization replaces P(T | pa(T)) with a point mass at T=t — this changes what the Σ operates on. The Σ itself is unchanged; what's summed over and with what weight is what changes.
Bridge insight: Bayesian inference and causal inference share the Σ as their fundamental operation. The difference is entirely about WHAT you condition on and WHAT you sum over. Bayes conditions on everything observed; causal inference selectively conditions only on the back-door set. Bayes sums over parameters with posterior weights; causal inference sums over confounders with population weights P(Z), deliberately NOT P(Z|T). This is why the math bridge shows that "Bayes never leaves Rung 1 by itself" — it has the engine (Σ) but lacks the map (the DAG that tells it what to condition on).

10. The ID Algorithm — When Σ Alone Is Not Enough

For complex graphs where no single adjustment set works, Shpitser & Pearl (2008) provide the ID algorithm — a complete polynomial-time procedure that expresses any identified causal effect as a sequence of Σ operations, possibly nested and interleaved with further conditioning.

The algorithm works by decomposing the graph into C-components (confounded components) and constructing a sequence of Σ operations that progressively marginalize over variables in each component. The output is a formula involving only observed probabilities and Σ — but the formula may be substantially more complex than the simple back-door adjustment.

Practical note: The ID algorithm is implemented in Ananke (Bhattacharya et al., UCLA) and dagitty. For most applied problems, the back-door or front-door criterion suffices. The ID algorithm is essential for complex longitudinal settings with time-varying confounding and treatment-confounder feedback.

11. Further Reading — The Frontier of Identification Research

11.1 Foundational Completeness Results

Shpitser & Pearl (2008) — The ID algorithm: a sound and complete polynomial-time procedure for identifying any causal effect from observational data + a known DAG. Huang & Valtorta (2006) independently proved do-calculus completeness.

11.2 Beyond Observational Data — General Identifiability (gID)

Lee, Correa & Bareinboim (2019) extended identification to settings with arbitrary combinations of observational AND interventional distributions (surrogate experiments). Their hedgelet/thicket structures characterize when effects are g-identifiable. Do-calculus is complete for gID (with positivity).

Kivva et al. (2022, 2024) revisited gID and showed a critical caveat: without positivity, do-calculus is NOT sound — you can derive identification formulas that are mathematically impossible to compute from the available data. This has profound implications for practical causal inference with finite samples.

11.3 Counterfactual Identification — Climbing to Rung 3

Correa, Lee & Bareinboim (2021) developed a sound and complete algorithm for identifying nested counterfactuals (e.g., natural direct effects) from arbitrary surrogate experiments. Their key theorem reduces nested counterfactual identification to non-nested identification. The M-ID algorithm (Maiti, Plecko & Bareinboim 2025) extends this to arbitrary counterfactual queries under monotonicity constraints.

11.4 IV & LATE — Modern Challenges

Blandhol, Bonney, Mogstad & Torgovitsky (2022) showed that 2SLS with covariates generally does NOT recover a convex-weighted LATE unless covariates are interacted with the instrument. Słoczyński (2022) demonstrated negative weights when first stages have opposite signs. Mogstad, Torgovitsky & Walters (2021) characterized what IV actually estimates with multiple instruments.

Ma (2025) proposed an orthogonalized Anderson-Rubin test for LATE with high-dimensional controls, uniformly valid regardless of identification strength.

11.5 DiD — The Heterogeneous Timing Revolution

Goodman-Bacon (2021) and de Chaisemartin & D'Haultfoeuille (2020) independently showed that two-way fixed effects (TWFE) with staggered adoption produces contaminated estimates. Callaway & Sant'Anna (2021) proposed doubly robust group-time ATTs. Roth (2022) showed pre-trends tests have low power — passing them is necessary but not sufficient.

11.6 Synthetic Control & SDID

Arkhangelsky, Athey, Hirshberg, Imbens & Wager (2021) proposed Synthetic Difference-in-Differences (SDID), combining SC's unit weighting with DiD's double-differencing. The method nests both DiD and SC as special cases.

11.7 Beyond Classical Probability — Categorical Identification

Cakiqi & Little (2024) reformulated causal identification in symmetric monoidal categories, enabling identification in settings where classical probability doesn't apply: relational databases, hardware description languages, distributed systems. They derive purely syntactic analogues of back-door and front-door adjustment.

11.8 Machine Learning for Identification

Chernozhukov et al. (2018) — Double/Debiased ML: Neyman-orthogonal scores + cross-fitting provides √N-consistent estimates with high-dimensional nuisances. Extends to LATE, DiD, and partially linear models.

Belloni et al. (2017) — DML for LATE with high-dimensional instruments.

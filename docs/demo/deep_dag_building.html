📐 Deep Dive: How Causal Graphs Are Built

From expert knowledge to algorithmic discovery to LLM-assisted elicitation — the full spectrum of methods for constructing causal DAGs.

1. Why This Matters

Building a causal graph is the hardest and most important step in causal inference. The graph determines what you adjust for, what you exclude, what's identifiable, and what's testable. Get it wrong, and every downstream estimate — no matter how sophisticated — is biased.

Unlike estimation (which can be automated), graph construction requires domain knowledge. No algorithm can tell you that "hunger" (a latent variable) drives both app-use and food orders — a human, familiar with how food-delivery platforms work, must encode that belief as edges in the DAG.

Key insight: The graph is a scientific model, not a statistical summary. You cannot learn it from data alone without assumptions — at best, data can tell you which graphs are consistent with observed patterns, not which one is correct.

3. Classical Methods for Building Causal Graphs

3.1 Expert Elicitation

The oldest and most reliable method: ask people who understand the system.

Domain experts — product managers, data scientists, engineers, subject-matter experts — draw arrows based on their understanding of how the system actually works. A PM knows the targeting algorithm uses app-use history (W→T). An engineer knows send-time jitter is randomized (Z→T). A data scientist knows engagement scores are computed from notifications and orders (T→S, Y→S).

Strengths: Encodes genuine causal knowledge; no algorithm can replace human understanding of mechanisms.
Limitations: Experts can disagree; cognitive biases (overconfidence, anchoring); doesn't scale to hundreds of variables. Best combined with data-driven validation.

3.2 Literature Review & Prior Evidence

Published studies, prior RCTs, and established theory constrain the graph. If prior experiments show that notifications increase app-opens, the edge T→M is supported by evidence — not just intuition. If no plausible physical mechanism exists for notifications to drain batteries, the absent edge T↛NC is a defensible, falsifiable claim.

Strengths: Evidence-based; reproducible; builds on cumulative scientific knowledge.
Limitations: Published studies may not generalize to your context (transportability problem); literature may be sparse for novel domains.

3.3 Constraint-Based Discovery — PC & FCI Algorithms

Starting from the data alone, constraint-based algorithms test conditional independence relations to infer graph structure. The core idea (Spirtes, Glymour & Scheines 1991):

  1. Start with a fully connected undirected graph.
  2. For each pair (X, Y), test whether X ⊥ Y | Z for some conditioning set Z. If yes, remove the edge X–Y — they are not directly connected.
  3. Orient edges using collider patterns: if X–Z–Y is in the graph, and Z was NOT conditioned on when X and Y became independent, then Z is a collider (X→Z←Y).

PC algorithm (Spirtes et al. 2000): Assumes no latent confounders. Fast and widely used.
FCI algorithm (Spirtes et al. 1995): Allows latent variables — correctly leaves U as latent rather than forcing an edge. More conservative but more honest.

Key limitation — Markov equivalence: PC cannot distinguish X→Y from X←Y from X↔Y (with latent) when they belong to the same Markov equivalence class. A graph with edges A→B→C is observationally indistinguishable from A←B←C — both imply A ⊥ C | B. You need additional assumptions (temporal order, intervention, domain knowledge) to orient edges.

3.4 Score-Based Discovery — GES

Score-based methods search over possible graph structures, scoring each by how well it fits the data using information criteria:

Greedy Equivalence Search (Chickering 2002): Start with an empty graph. Greedily add edges that improve the score (forward phase), then remove edges that no longer help (backward phase). Repeat until convergence. The result is the highest-scoring Markov equivalence class.

Strengths: Scalable to many variables; clear optimization objective.
Limitations: Scores measure fit, not causation. A well-fitting graph may still get causal directions wrong. Like PC, GES returns an equivalence class, not a fully oriented DAG.

3.5 Functional Causal Discovery — LiNGAM

If you are willing to assume linear, non-Gaussian structural equations, LiNGAM (Shimizu et al. 2006) can uniquely identify the full DAG — including edge directions — from observational data alone. The non-Gaussian assumption breaks the Markov equivalence: the asymmetry in noise distributions reveals causal direction.

Strengths: Full DAG identification without interventions; unique solution.
Limitations: Strong parametric assumptions (linearity, non-Gaussian noise); doesn't handle latent confounders well.

4. Agentic AI & LLM-Powered Methods

4.1 LLM-Assisted Causal Elicitation

Large language models, prompted with domain context (e.g., "a food-delivery platform's notification system"), can propose candidate causal graphs by drawing on patterns from their training data. The LLM might suggest:

  • "Hunger likely confounds both app-use and orders — these should share a latent parent."
  • "Engagement scores are typically computed from user actions — they are likely colliders, not confounders."
  • "Weather and calendar variables affect both notification strategy and order behavior — they are likely confounders."

These suggestions are hypotheses to be validated, not final answers. But they dramatically accelerate the elicitation process by providing a structured starting point that domain experts can critique, refine, and correct.

Key references: Kıcıman et al. (2024) "Causal Reasoning and Large Language Models"; Long et al. (2024) "CausalGPT"; Darvariu et al. (2025) "LLMs for Causal Graph Construction."

Critical caveat: LLMs can hallucinate causal relationships that sound plausible but are false. Every LLM-proposed edge must be validated against domain knowledge and, where possible, data. The LLM is an accelerator, not an oracle.

4.2 Agentic Causal Discovery Loops

An LLM-powered agent iteratively refines a causal graph through a structured loop:

  1. Propose: Based on variable names, descriptions, and domain context, the agent proposes candidate edges with natural-language justifications.
  2. Test: Each proposed edge is tested against the data using conditional independence tests. Edges that imply testable constraints (d-separation → CI) are empirically evaluated.
  3. Revise: Edges that fail empirical tests are removed or re-oriented. The agent explains why each revision was made in natural language.
  4. Explain: The agent produces an audit trail: which edges were proposed, which survived testing, which were rejected, and the evidence for each decision.

This combines the scalability of automated discovery with the interpretability of human judgment. The human expert reviews the agent's output and provides feedback, closing the loop.

4.3 LLM + Knowledge Graphs (KGs)

When the domain has an existing knowledge graph — e.g., a company ontology linking "notification" → "user engagement" → "order" — the LLM can ground its causal hypotheses in structured, versioned knowledge:

  • The KG provides vocabulary and known relationships — a machine-readable map of what the organization already knows.
  • The LLM fills gaps — proposing causal directions for relationships that are correlated in the KG but not causally annotated.
  • The resulting DAG is version-controlled alongside the KG — every edge has provenance (human expert, LLM proposal, data-driven test, or combination).

This approach is particularly valuable in enterprise settings where knowledge is distributed across teams, documents, and databases.

5. Synthesis — How to Build a Graph in Practice

In practice, graph construction is iterative and multi-method. A realistic workflow:

  1. Start with expert elicitation — get the obvious edges right using domain knowledge.
  2. Augment with literature — anchor disputed edges in published evidence.
  3. Use LLMs to propose candidates — let the model suggest edges you might have missed. Review critically.
  4. Test with constraint-based discovery — run PC/FCI on data to see which proposed edges are consistent with observed independence patterns. Remove edges that are empirically contradicted.
  5. Validate absent edges — every edge you did NOT draw is a claim. Test it. If the data says two variables are dependent even after conditioning, your absent-edge claim may be wrong.
  6. Version control everything — the graph is an assumption artifact. When it changes, record why, and recompile all downstream results (adjustment sets, estimates, monitors).
Core principle: A causal graph is never "finished" — it is a living hypothesis about how the world works. It must be testable (via absent edges), versioned (so estimates are traceable), and evolvable (so new evidence can revise it). The NomNom DAG has 54 absent edges — each one is an invitation to falsify.

7. Graph Evaluation — Is Your DAG Any Good?

Once built, a causal graph should be evaluated against several criteria beyond just "it feels right":

7.1 Testable Implications

Every absent edge implies a conditional independence: if there is no edge X→Y, then X ⊥ Y | pa(Y) in some conditioning set. These implications can be empirically tested against data. A graph whose absent-edge implications are consistently violated by the data is falsified — it must be revised.

7.2 Stability Across Environments

A correct causal graph should be stable: the conditional distribution P(effect | direct causes) should remain invariant across environments (Peters, Bühlmann & Meinshausen 2016). If P(Y | pa(Y)) changes dramatically when the data collection process changes, the parent set is likely wrong.

7.3 Simplicity (Occam's Razor)

Among graphs that are equally consistent with the data and domain knowledge, prefer the simpler one — fewer edges, fewer latent variables. But simplicity is a tiebreaker, not a primary criterion: a graph that is too simple to explain known mechanisms is underfit.

7.4 Predictive Accuracy

While the graph is a causal model (not a predictive one), a graph whose implied conditional distributions fit the data poorly is suspicious. Use held-out log-likelihood or BIC to compare candidate graphs.

7.5 Inter-rater Reliability

Have multiple independent experts draw the graph for the same domain. Where they agree, confidence is higher. Where they disagree, those edges need investigation — they are the highest-value targets for experiments or data-driven testing.

8. Classic Examples Beyond NomNom

The graph-construction principles above apply universally. Here are three classic causal graphs from other domains that illustrate the same challenges:

Smoking → Lung Cancer (Fisher vs. Doll & Hill, 1950s). Fisher proposed a latent genetic confounder G causing both smoking desire and cancer susceptibility — the graph had edges G→Smoking and G→Cancer. Doll & Hill argued the evidence favored Smoking→Cancer directly, with no latent confounder. The debate hinged on an absent edge: was there a direct Smoking→Cancer link, or was it entirely confounded? Resolution required decades of evidence — illustrating that graph construction is a scientific process, not a one-shot statistical exercise.

HRT → Heart Disease (Nurses' Health Study vs. WHI RCT, 1990s-2000s). Observational studies found hormone replacement therapy protective against heart disease. The DAG omitted a key confounder: socioeconomic status (SES), which causes both HRT use (wealthier women access healthcare) and heart health (wealthier women have better outcomes). The WHI randomized trial later found HRT increased heart disease risk — the observational graph was wrong because it missed the SES confounder. This is why sensitivity analysis (E-value) is critical: it quantifies how strong an omitted confounder would need to be.

Education → Earnings (Griliches, 1970s). Does more schooling cause higher wages? The graph must contend with unobserved ability (A) causing both education and earnings (A→Ed, A→Wage), family background confounding, and measurement error in schooling. Griliches used instrumental variables (family background as instrument for education) — a graph-based identification strategy — to estimate the causal effect. The graph drove the choice of method.
